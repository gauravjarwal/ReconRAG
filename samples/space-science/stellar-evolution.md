# Stellar Evolution

## Star Formation

Stars form within giant molecular clouds (GMCs) — vast regions of cold, dense gas and dust spanning 50-300 light-years with masses of 10^3 to 10^7 solar masses. A localized region within the cloud undergoes gravitational collapse when its mass exceeds the Jeans mass, the critical threshold where gravitational potential energy overcomes thermal kinetic energy and internal gas pressure.

The Jeans mass depends on temperature and density: M_J = (5kT / GmH)^(3/2) × (3 / 4π ρ)^(1/2), where k is Boltzmann's constant, T is temperature, G is gravitational constant, mH is hydrogen mass, and ρ is density. For a typical molecular cloud at 10 K, the Jeans mass is approximately 1-10 solar masses.

As the cloud fragment contracts, it heats up and forms a protostar at its center. The protostar continues accreting material from a surrounding accretion disk. Bipolar jets — collimated outflows perpendicular to the disk — carry away angular momentum. This T Tauri phase lasts approximately 10 million years for a solar-mass star.

Nuclear fusion ignites when the core temperature reaches approximately 10 million Kelvin, initiating the proton-proton chain (for stars less than 1.3 solar masses) or the CNO cycle (for more massive stars). At this point, the star reaches the zero-age main sequence (ZAMS) on the Hertzsprung-Russell diagram.

## Main Sequence Lifetime

The main sequence is the longest phase of stellar evolution, during which hydrogen fuses to helium in the core. The star exists in hydrostatic equilibrium — gravitational contraction is balanced by radiation pressure from nuclear fusion.

Main sequence lifetime scales inversely with mass to roughly the 2.5 power: t_MS ≈ t_sun × (M/M_sun)^(-2.5). This means massive stars burn through their fuel dramatically faster:

- 0.5 M_sun (red dwarf): ~56 billion years (far exceeding the current age of the universe)
- 1.0 M_sun (Sun): ~10 billion years
- 2.0 M_sun (A-type star): ~1.8 billion years
- 10 M_sun (B-type star): ~20 million years
- 25 M_sun (O-type star): ~6 million years

The mass-luminosity relation for main sequence stars follows L ∝ M^3.5 approximately. A 10 solar mass star is roughly 3,000 times more luminous than the Sun but has only 10 times the fuel — hence the dramatically shorter lifetime.

## Post-Main Sequence: Low and Intermediate Mass Stars (< 8 M_sun)

When hydrogen is exhausted in the core, fusion ceases and the core contracts under gravity. This heats the hydrogen shell surrounding the inert helium core, igniting hydrogen shell burning. The star's outer envelope expands and cools, and the star ascends the red giant branch (RGB) on the HR diagram.

For stars above ~0.5 M_sun, the helium core eventually reaches 100 million Kelvin, igniting helium fusion via the triple-alpha process (three helium-4 nuclei fuse to carbon-12). In low-mass stars (~0.8-2 M_sun), helium ignition occurs degenerately as a helium flash — a violent but brief thermonuclear runaway in the electron-degenerate core. The star then settles onto the horizontal branch, fusing helium steadily.

After helium exhaustion, the star develops a carbon-oxygen core surrounded by helium and hydrogen burning shells. It ascends the asymptotic giant branch (AGB), pulsating thermally and shedding its outer layers through intense stellar winds (mass loss rates of 10^-7 to 10^-4 solar masses per year). These ejected layers form a planetary nebula — a shell of ionized gas illuminated by the hot exposed core.

The remnant core, now a white dwarf, has a mass of ~0.5-1.4 M_sun compressed into an Earth-sized volume. White dwarfs are supported against gravitational collapse by electron degeneracy pressure. The Chandrasekhar limit (1.4 M_sun) is the maximum mass a white dwarf can sustain — above this, electron degeneracy pressure cannot prevent further collapse.

## Post-Main Sequence: Massive Stars (> 8 M_sun)

Massive stars undergo successive stages of nuclear burning, each producing heavier elements and lasting progressively shorter periods:

- Hydrogen burning: ~7 million years
- Helium burning: ~700,000 years
- Carbon burning: ~600 years
- Neon burning: ~1 year
- Oxygen burning: ~6 months
- Silicon burning: ~1 day

Silicon burning produces iron-56 (Fe-56), the most tightly bound nucleus. Fusing elements heavier than iron is endothermic — it consumes energy rather than releasing it. The iron core grows until it exceeds the Chandrasekhar limit, at which point electron degeneracy pressure fails.

Core collapse occurs in milliseconds. The inner core collapses to nuclear density (10^14 g/cm3), rebounds, and sends a shock wave outward. Neutrinos carry away ~99% of the gravitational binding energy (~3 × 10^46 joules). The shock wave, revived by neutrino heating, expels the outer layers in a core-collapse supernova (Type II for hydrogen-rich, Type Ib/Ic for stripped stars).

The remnant depends on mass:
- 8-20 M_sun progenitor: Neutron star (1.4-2.1 M_sun, ~10 km radius, supported by neutron degeneracy pressure). Rotation rates up to 716 Hz (millisecond pulsars).
- >20-25 M_sun progenitor: Black hole (stellar mass, 5-50+ M_sun). The Tolman-Oppenheimer-Volkoff limit (~2.1-2.5 M_sun) is the maximum neutron star mass — above this, collapse to a black hole is inevitable.
