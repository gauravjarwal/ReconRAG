# Cosmology

## The Big Bang and the Early Universe

The Big Bang theory describes the universe's origin from an extremely hot, dense state approximately 13.8 billion years ago. This age is determined independently by multiple methods: cosmic microwave background (CMB) measurements, the Hubble constant extrapolation, and nucleosynthesis constraints on the baryon-to-photon ratio.

### Timeline of the Early Universe

**Planck Epoch (0 to 10^-43 seconds):** All four fundamental forces (gravity, strong nuclear, weak nuclear, electromagnetic) were unified. Quantum gravity effects dominated. No current theory adequately describes this era — it requires a theory of quantum gravity that unifies general relativity and quantum mechanics.

**Grand Unification Epoch (10^-43 to 10^-36 seconds):** Gravity separated from the other three forces. The strong, weak, and electromagnetic forces remained unified as a single force described by Grand Unified Theories (GUTs).

**Inflationary Epoch (10^-36 to 10^-32 seconds):** The universe underwent exponential expansion, increasing in size by a factor of at least 10^26 in a fraction of a second. Inflation, proposed by Alan Guth in 1980, solves three classical problems:
- **Horizon problem:** Why is the CMB uniform to 1 part in 100,000 across regions that were never in causal contact? Because they WERE in contact before inflation stretched them apart.
- **Flatness problem:** Why is the universe's geometry so close to flat (Ω ≈ 1)? Inflation drives any initial curvature toward flatness, like inflating a balloon makes its surface appear flat locally.
- **Monopole problem:** Why haven't magnetic monopoles (predicted by GUTs) been observed? Inflation diluted their density to negligible levels.

**Quark-Gluon Plasma (10^-12 to 10^-6 seconds):** The universe was a soup of free quarks and gluons. As it cooled below ~2 × 10^12 K, quarks combined into hadrons (protons and neutrons) — a phase transition called quark confinement, now studied experimentally at CERN's Large Hadron Collider and Brookhaven's RHIC.

**Big Bang Nucleosynthesis (BBN) (10 seconds to 20 minutes):** Protons and neutrons fused to form light nuclei. The predicted abundances — 75% hydrogen (by mass), 25% helium-4, trace deuterium (~0.01%), and trace lithium-7 (~10^-10) — match observations with remarkable precision, providing one of the strongest confirmations of the Big Bang model. The deuterium abundance is particularly sensitive to the baryon density, constraining ordinary matter to ~5% of the universe's total energy budget.

**Recombination and the CMB (380,000 years):** The universe cooled to ~3,000 K, allowing electrons to combine with nuclei to form neutral atoms. Photons decoupled from matter and streamed freely — these photons are the cosmic microwave background radiation we observe today, redshifted to a blackbody temperature of 2.725 K. The CMB's tiny temperature fluctuations (±200 microKelvin) encode the density perturbations that seeded all large-scale structure in the universe.

## Dark Matter

Approximately 27% of the universe's energy content consists of dark matter — matter that interacts gravitationally but does not emit, absorb, or reflect electromagnetic radiation. Evidence comes from multiple independent observations:

**Galaxy rotation curves:** Stars in the outer regions of spiral galaxies orbit at velocities far higher than predicted by the visible mass distribution. The flat rotation curve (velocity remains constant at large radii instead of declining as v ∝ r^(-1/2)) implies a massive dark matter halo extending well beyond the visible disk. First measured systematically by Vera Rubin in the 1970s.

**Gravitational lensing:** Massive galaxy clusters bend light from background galaxies, creating arcs and multiple images. The total mass inferred from lensing exceeds the visible mass by a factor of 5-10. The Bullet Cluster (1E 0657-558) provides the most direct evidence: two colliding galaxy clusters show the dark matter (mapped by lensing) separated from the hot gas (mapped by X-ray emission), demonstrating that dark matter is collisionless.

**CMB power spectrum:** The relative heights of acoustic peaks in the CMB angular power spectrum precisely constrain the dark matter density. The third peak, in particular, is sensitive to the ratio of dark matter to baryonic matter.

**Leading dark matter candidates:**
- WIMPs (Weakly Interacting Massive Particles): Hypothetical particles with masses ~10-1000 GeV interacting via the weak force. Despite decades of direct detection experiments (LUX, XENON1T, PandaX), no definitive signal has been found, pushing WIMP cross-section limits below 10^-47 cm2.
- Axions: Ultra-light particles (mass ~10^-5 eV) originally proposed to solve the strong CP problem in QCD. ADMX (Axion Dark Matter eXperiment) searches for axion-photon conversion in a strong magnetic field.
- Sterile neutrinos: Hypothetical neutrinos that interact only gravitationally. Could explain certain X-ray line observations from galaxy clusters.
- Primordial black holes: Black holes formed in the early universe rather than from stellar collapse. Constraints from microlensing surveys and CMB distortions limit their contribution but have not fully excluded them in certain mass ranges.

## Dark Energy

Dark energy comprises approximately 68% of the universe's energy density and drives the accelerating expansion of the universe, discovered in 1998 by two independent teams (the Supernova Cosmology Project and the High-z Supernova Search Team, awarded the 2011 Nobel Prize in Physics).

Type Ia supernovae serve as standardizable candles — their peak luminosity correlates with their light curve width (the Phillips relation). By measuring apparent brightness versus redshift for distant Type Ia SNe, both teams found that supernovae at redshift z ≈ 0.5 were ~25% fainter than expected in a decelerating universe, indicating the expansion rate is increasing.

**The Cosmological Constant (Λ):** The simplest model — vacuum energy with equation of state w = P/(ρc^2) = -1 exactly. Consistent with all current observations but presents the cosmological constant problem: quantum field theory predicts a vacuum energy density 10^120 times larger than observed. This is arguably the worst prediction in the history of physics.

**The ΛCDM Model:** The standard cosmological model combines dark energy (Λ) with cold dark matter (CDM). Its six parameters — baryon density, dark matter density, dark energy density, amplitude and tilt of primordial fluctuations, and optical depth to reionization — fit the CMB power spectrum, baryon acoustic oscillations (BAO), and Type Ia supernova distances with extraordinary precision.

Current composition of the universe:
- Dark energy: 68.3%
- Dark matter: 26.8%
- Ordinary (baryonic) matter: 4.9%
- Radiation: <0.01%

The Hubble tension — a 4-5 sigma discrepancy between the Hubble constant measured locally (H0 ≈ 73 km/s/Mpc from Cepheids and supernovae) and from the early universe (H0 ≈ 67.4 km/s/Mpc from Planck CMB data) — may hint at new physics beyond ΛCDM, or may reflect systematic errors in one or both measurement methods. The James Webb Space Telescope's Cepheid observations have so far confirmed the local measurement, deepening the tension.

## The Fate of the Universe

The universe's ultimate fate depends on the nature of dark energy:

**If w = -1 (cosmological constant):** The universe expands forever at an accelerating rate. Galaxies beyond the Local Group recede past the cosmic event horizon and become unobservable. Over trillions of years: stars exhaust their fuel, stellar remnants cool to background temperature, proton decay (if it occurs, with half-life ~10^34 years) dissolves all baryonic matter, and black holes evaporate via Hawking radiation on timescales of 10^67 years (stellar mass) to 10^100 years (supermassive). The universe approaches maximum entropy — heat death.

**If w < -1 (phantom energy):** The expansion rate increases without bound. The scale factor diverges in finite time — the Big Rip. All bound structures are torn apart in reverse order of binding strength: galaxy clusters, galaxies, star systems, planets, atoms, and finally spacetime itself. For w = -1.5, the Big Rip would occur approximately 22 billion years from now.

**If w > -1 and time-varying:** Dark energy could weaken over cosmic time, potentially allowing gravity to eventually dominate and reverse expansion — leading to a Big Crunch, the time-reverse of the Big Bang. Current data slightly favors w close to but not exactly -1, and several recent analyses (DESI BAO results, 2024) suggest w may be evolving, though the evidence is not yet definitive.
