# Planetary Science

## Planet Classification

The International Astronomical Union (IAU) defines a planet as a celestial body that: (1) orbits the Sun, (2) has sufficient mass for self-gravity to achieve hydrostatic equilibrium (nearly round shape), and (3) has cleared the neighborhood around its orbit. This 2006 definition famously reclassified Pluto as a dwarf planet.

### Terrestrial Planets

The four inner planets — Mercury, Venus, Earth, and Mars — are characterized by solid rocky surfaces, iron-nickel cores, and relatively thin or absent atmospheres.

**Mercury:** Smallest planet (0.055 Earth masses). No atmosphere to speak of — surface temperatures swing from 430°C (dayside) to -180°C (nightside). Enormous iron core comprising ~75% of its radius, giving it the highest uncompressed density of any planet (5.3 g/cm3). The MESSENGER mission discovered water ice in permanently shadowed polar craters despite the extreme surface temperatures.

**Venus:** Often called Earth's twin in size (0.815 Earth masses) but radically different in conditions. Atmosphere is 96.5% CO2 with surface pressure of 92 atmospheres — equivalent to being 900 meters underwater on Earth. Runaway greenhouse effect maintains surface temperature of 465°C, hotter than Mercury despite being farther from the Sun. The planet rotates retrograde (clockwise when viewed from above the north pole) with a sidereal day of 243 Earth days — longer than its orbital period of 225 days.

**Earth:** The only known planet with liquid surface water, active plate tectonics, and a strong magnetic dynamo driven by convection in the liquid iron outer core. The magnetosphere extends ~65,000 km sunward and millions of kilometers in the magnetotail, shielding the atmosphere from solar wind stripping. Without it, Earth would likely have lost its atmosphere similarly to Mars.

**Mars:** Half Earth's diameter with a thin CO2 atmosphere (surface pressure ~0.6% of Earth's). Evidence of ancient liquid water is abundant: valley networks, lake deposits in Gale Crater (explored by Curiosity), and recurring slope lineae suggesting seasonal briny flows. The dichotomy between the smooth northern lowlands and heavily cratered southern highlands remains one of Mars science's great puzzles — possibly caused by a giant impact early in the planet's history.

### Gas Giants and Ice Giants

**Jupiter:** The largest planet at 317.8 Earth masses, composed primarily of hydrogen and helium. The Great Red Spot is an anticyclonic storm larger than Earth, persisting for at least 350 years. Jupiter's magnetic field is 20,000 times stronger than Earth's, creating intense radiation belts that would deliver a lethal dose to an unshielded human in minutes. The Galilean moons — Io (most volcanically active body in the solar system), Europa (subsurface ocean beneath ice shell), Ganymede (largest moon, larger than Mercury, has its own magnetosphere), and Callisto (most heavily cratered body known) — constitute a miniature planetary system.

**Saturn:** Famous for its extensive ring system, composed primarily of water ice particles ranging from micrometer-sized grains to house-sized boulders. The rings are remarkably thin — spanning 280,000 km in diameter but only 10-30 meters thick. Cassini mission data suggests the rings are young (100-200 million years old) rather than primordial, possibly formed from a disrupted moon or captured comet. Titan, Saturn's largest moon, is the only moon with a dense atmosphere (1.5 atm surface pressure, primarily nitrogen) and has lakes of liquid methane and ethane on its surface — the only known stable surface liquids beyond Earth.

**Uranus and Neptune (Ice Giants):** Distinguished from gas giants by their composition — approximately 80% water, methane, and ammonia ices by mass, with only thin hydrogen-helium envelopes. Uranus has an extreme axial tilt of 97.77° — it essentially orbits on its side, possibly due to a giant impact during formation. Neptune has the strongest sustained winds of any planet, reaching 2,100 km/h. Triton, Neptune's largest moon, orbits retrograde — likely a captured Kuiper Belt object.

## Exoplanet Detection Methods

The study of planets beyond our solar system has revolutionized planetary science since the first confirmed exoplanet discovery in 1995 (51 Pegasi b).

**Transit Method:** When a planet crosses (transits) its host star as seen from Earth, it blocks a fraction of the starlight. The transit depth gives the planet's radius: ΔF/F = (R_planet/R_star)^2. A Jupiter-sized planet transiting a Sun-sized star causes ~1% dimming; an Earth-sized planet causes ~0.01% dimming. NASA's Kepler mission (2009-2018) discovered over 2,600 confirmed exoplanets using this method. TESS (Transiting Exoplanet Survey Satellite) continues the census, focusing on nearby bright stars.

**Radial Velocity (Doppler) Method:** A planet's gravitational pull causes its host star to wobble, producing periodic Doppler shifts in the star's spectral lines. The velocity semi-amplitude gives the planet's minimum mass (M_planet × sin i, where i is orbital inclination). High-precision spectrographs like ESPRESSO at the VLT achieve ~10 cm/s precision — sufficient to detect Earth-mass planets in the habitable zone of Sun-like stars.

**Direct Imaging:** Photographing exoplanets by blocking the host star's light with a coronagraph or starshade. Extremely challenging due to the contrast ratio (~10^-10 for an Earth analog around a Sun-like star) and small angular separation. Currently limited to young, massive planets at wide separations (>10 AU) that are still self-luminous from formation heat. The Roman Space Telescope's coronagraph and the proposed Habitable Worlds Observatory aim to directly image Earth-like exoplanets.

**Gravitational Microlensing:** A foreground star's gravity bends light from a background star, creating a magnification event. If the lensing star has a planet, it produces a brief additional spike in the light curve. Sensitive to planets at 1-10 AU from their host star, including free-floating (rogue) planets not bound to any star. Estimates suggest billions of rogue planets in the Milky Way alone.

## Habitability

The habitable zone (HZ) is the region around a star where a rocky planet with sufficient atmospheric pressure could sustain liquid water on its surface. For our Sun, the conservative HZ spans approximately 0.95-1.67 AU. The HZ boundaries depend on stellar luminosity and spectral type:

- M-dwarf stars: HZ at 0.1-0.4 AU. Planets are tidally locked (one side permanently facing the star). Subject to intense stellar flares that may strip atmospheres.
- G-type stars (Sun-like): HZ at ~0.95-1.7 AU. Best studied and most Earth-like conditions expected.
- A-type stars: HZ at 2-4 AU. Main sequence lifetime too short (<2 billion years) for complex life to evolve based on Earth's timeline.

Beyond the habitable zone, subsurface oceans maintained by tidal heating offer alternative habitability. Europa and Enceladus are prime candidates — Enceladus's geysers (discovered by Cassini) eject water vapor containing organic molecules, molecular hydrogen, and silica nanoparticles, suggesting hydrothermal vents on the ocean floor that could support chemosynthetic life.
